Regions turn at different times

What it means

Countries differ mainly in when they turn, not how: given its own clock, one curve fits 94%, and regions differ clearly in timing (p < 0.001). A later clock is not a worse country.

Fit on 1950–2023 only · after SITAR growth curves (Cole et al., 2010) · * 16 of 200 separate fits hit their limits · tests: 115 well-observed countries

Our first try fitted an S-shaped curve to each country separately. It fit, but gave nonsense: for Japan, whose decline happened before 1950, it said women started at nine children. So we borrowed the SITAR idea from children’s growth charts: one shared S-curve, and one number per country — its turning point, the year fertility falls fastest. Watch what happens when we shift each country. By calendar year, time explains 27 percent of the differences between countries. Shifted to their own turning points, one curve explains 94 percent; a level shift with the same number of parameters reaches only 87. In backtests from 1983, 1993 and 2003, the shared curve had the smallest typical error twenty years ahead. On the map, the clock runs from Europe to Africa, and a Kruskal–Wallis test confirms that regions turn at different times. In 2023, Nigeria is at its turning point, Türkiye passed it about forty years ago, Japan about ninety.
