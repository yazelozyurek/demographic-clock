What it means

No wealth threshold sets the clock. Health moves with it: in 94% of countries, life expectancy rose fastest first, a median 18 years earlier. These are patterns, not causes.

External data: income from the Maddison Project Database 2023 (GDP per person, via Our World in Data) · schooling from Barro–Lee v3, women 25–34
Health clock: same shared-curve method on UN WPP life expectancy, 1950–2023 · many other candidates screened (FDR) · association, not cause

First, income or education, on the left. GDP per person at the turning point ranged from 1,100 to 24,000 dollars. As a threshold it missed the year by 14 years, worse than guessing the typical year. Women’s education was steadier, but no better than the calendar. That no is a result, not a failure, and it led us to transition theory’s oldest prediction: does health come first? We measured a second clock, the year life expectancy rose fastest, with the same shared curve. Above the line, health came first: in 94 percent of 115 countries, a median of 18 years earlier, and the two clocks move together, rho 0.77. We screened many candidates, with multiple-test correction. So income does not set the clock; health moves with it. But first is not the same as cause: a rooster crows before every sunrise, yet it does not make the sun rise.
