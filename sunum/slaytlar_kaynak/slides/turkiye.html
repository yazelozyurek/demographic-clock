What it means

Where the 1940s medicines saved more lives, births first rose, but children per woman fell further by 1990. One of three tests, each planned in advance, supports health as a cause; none settles it.

Natural experiment: Kreitmeir & Überfuhr (2024) instrument, after Acemoglu & Johnson (2007) · two-stage least squares, weak-IV-robust 95% CI
Each test's plan was committed before its results · HIV: World Bank (UNAIDS) · Türkiye: UN WPP 2024 intervals · ASA Ethical Guidelines (2022)

So, one more question: is health a cause, or just the rooster? We ran three tests, each planned before seeing its results. Two could not tell. The third is a natural experiment: in the 1940s, new medicines and vaccines reached the world at once, and countries hit hardest by those diseases gained most. Where they saved more lives, more babies were born, but each woman had fewer: about ten percent more life expectancy, 0.44 fewer children by 1990. This supports health as a cause, but does not settle it, because the signal comes from how sick countries were in 1940. Türkiye’s health clock turned in 1965, its fertility clock in 1982. Its population likely peaks in 2047; for 2100 we show a range, 26 to 125 million. Our limits: these are country patterns, not facts about individual women, and the clock is not a race.
